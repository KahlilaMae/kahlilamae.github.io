How We Think

*Design Notes · Draft*

What happens after a decision is made?

A new policy can reach every team while the reasoning behind it gets lost along the way. Employees are left to work out how to apply the decision when the situation falls outside the examples they were given. Managers may only discover what the guidance missed after it causes a delay.

*How We Think* explores how a short scenario can help employees and managers understand each other’s reasoning during a rollout. The employee hears why the decision was made. The manager hears what applying it would mean for the people doing the work.

The billing scenario is used in the preview. The other scenarios are proposed examples.

Billing policy rollout

Leadership has decided that billing credits above $500 require manager approval so large credits are handled consistently. An employee is working on a $650 credit. Their manager is out, and the customer’s payment is scheduled for that evening.

**Individual contributor.** The employee responds aloud before seeing the manager’s perspective. “I’d wait for approval, but the customer’s payment is scheduled for tonight. Is there someone else who can approve it?”

**Manager.** The employee then hears the actual manager explain why the approval threshold exists. The employee can see the reason for the policy. The manager can see that the guidance leaves the team without a clear next step when an approval is time-sensitive.

Feedback loop between employee and manager

The employee explains how they would apply the policy before hearing the manager’s reasoning. The manager shares why larger credits require approval and hears where the employee would get stuck. After hearing each other, the employee explains what they would do differently, and the manager confirms what guidance the team can use.

In the billing scenario, the employee would flag the payment deadline immediately. The manager would name a backup approver and share the updated guidance with the team. A fresh scenario could test whether the employee can use that approval path. If a step is still unclear, the employee can flag it and the manager can clarify the guidance again.

Role of the LLM

The LLM brings relevant excerpts from the two spoken responses together and suggests a question for discussion. Each person can review how their reasoning is represented. Feedback comes from the people involved, and the manager owns any change to the guidance.

Supporting business goals and impact

*How We Think* is designed to shorten the time between announcing a policy and applying it consistently. The feedback loop gives employees a way to surface unclear steps and managers a chance to address them early in the rollout, before they lead to delays and rework.

In the billing scenario, naming a backup approver gives employees a way to handle urgent credits while following the policy. If more credits are applied before payments are processed, the team may spend less time handling repeat contacts and payment corrections. That is the financial opportunity in this example.

Business goalWhat a pilot could measure
Put the policy into practice soonerDays from announcement until the team consistently routes credits above $500 to an authorized approver and documents approval
Reduce the cost of billing reworkStaff time spent on repeat contacts and payment corrections linked to late credits

A pilot could compare these measures across teams rolling out the same policy with and without the scenario, if feasible. Multiply any reduction in rework hours by the fully loaded hourly labor cost to estimate the value of staff time freed up. Compare that estimate with the time and cost of running the exercise.
